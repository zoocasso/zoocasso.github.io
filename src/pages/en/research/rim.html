At a glance

The ICCAS 2026 Blender scene, rendered with the paper's script from 8 × 8 camera positions, changing only the number of views and the resolution (the paper uses 6 × 6). Each view is rendered at 1920 × 1080 and the 1000 × 1000 area around the objects is used without downscaling (500 × 500 on phones). Reconstruction shifts the 64 views by distance and averages them, precomputed in 31 steps from 0.36 to 1.55 m. Both move by themselves until you use the mouse or the slider, and then stay where you leave them.

Problem

A lens array camera divides its sensor pixels between the number of lenses and the pixels under each lens. More lenses give richer depth information, but each lens then forms an elemental image only a few pixels wide. As it is, the image is too coarse both for people to look at and for a detector to work on.

What a lens array records

In a lens array camera, each lens with the patch of sensor behind it works like a tiny camera; the home page lab shows it with camera models. More precisely, the light from one point passes several lenses and lands at a different spot (pixel) under each. The lens index is where the light passed, and the pixel position under the lens is the direction it came from, so one elemental image holds both position and direction.

RIM: ray-based inverse mapping

Think of each pixel of the elemental image as re-emitted through a virtual lens array and captured again by a virtual camera. Under telecentric capture and orthographic recapture, this turns into a one-to-one swap of (lens index, position under the lens) into (position under the lens, lens index). There is no interpolation and therefore no quantization error, and it produces as many views as there are pixels under a lens, each with as many pixels as there are lenses. The paper uses the result as the first stage of computational integral imaging reconstruction (CIIR).

iRIM: a virtual lens array from multi-view images

Because RIM is one to one, it also runs backwards. iRIM takes multi-view images and synthesizes the elemental image of the virtual lens array you want. The paper covers a matched 6 × 6 configuration for a 24-inch FHD panel (1.6056 mm lenses, 320 × 180 of them).

How the home page lab is made

The lab computes lens array capture, RIM and reconstruction in the browser and animates them. The samples are a 3D scene captured in the browser and a light field captured with a real camera (Lytro). At the end the reconstruction plane is shown head on, and clicking an object moves the focus to its depth.

  • 3D scene: the monkey head and the torus exported from the ICCAS 2026 paper's Blender scene stand as real 3D models, and the lens array capture is computed in the browser. In this lab's pinhole model, the pixel at the same spot under every lens looks in the same direction, so one view is an oblique parallel projection of the scene. The 8 × 8 views are drawn that way, with their depth, and rearrangement and reconstruction are computed from them. The monkey head stands so that it hides part of the torus. The spatial resolution is 64 × 64 lenses. The empty background is not set up as a wall in space; its light is shown from the lens array on. In the result, clicking an object moves the focus to the median depth of the four central views around the click.
  • Capture: a pinhole array model. Each elemental-image pixel starts at the 3D position of the point it recorded (from the depth above) and travels through its lens to the sensor. The camera models in step 1 show that a lens with the sensor patch behind it is a tiny camera; they are drawn larger than a lens so they can be seen. How the light actually passes the lenses is shown above, in "What a lens array records".
  • Rearrangement and reconstruction: RIM, which swaps (lens index, position under the lens), and reconstruction, which shifts the views by distance and averages them, follow the papers. When the distance matches an object's depth, its samples land back where they came from and it becomes sharp. On screen the reconstruction plane is drawn at the depth that distance stands for, so an object turns sharp as the plane passes where it stood.
  • Lytro sample: the occlusions_26 scene from the Stanford Lytro Light Field Archive (A. S. Raj, M. Lowney, R. Shah, G. Wetzstein, 2016). The lenslet image the archive provides is not the raw sensor image: Lytro's software has demosaiced it, aligned and resampled it to a square grid and corrected distortion, giving 14 × 14 pixels under each of 541 × 376 lenses (the archive labels these files "RAW"). 128 × 128 lenses where a branch hides part of a stop sign are cut from it without resampling, and the central 8 × 8 pixels (= 8 × 8 views) are used, leaving out the empty or dark outer band (phones: 2 × 2 lenses averaged, 64 × 64). The 16-bit values are tone-mapped once for the whole picture to 8-bit for display. This photo has no 3D shape, so step 1 does not simulate a capture: it shows the recorded lenslet image on the sensor as it is and zooms into it. Rearrangement moves pixels without altering them, and reconstruction shifts and averages the 64 views (computed, not optical blur). Clicking the picture moves the focus to the plane where the views agree best around that spot; where there is no texture and the views match about equally at every depth, no focus is chosen. "Estimated depth layers" draws, from the same 64 views, how well they agree on each of 25 planes of a plane sweep (view variance turned into weights by a softmax). It is not measured 3D, and the depth axis is disparity (px/view), not metres. The archive states no licence, so the data is credited and only a crop is used, for a non-commercial demonstration.
  • Sound: the music, sound effects and robot voice are all synthesized by the browser.
