Problem

A lens array camera divides its sensor pixels between the number of lenses and the pixels under each lens. More lenses give richer depth information, but each lens then forms an elemental image only a few pixels wide. As it is, the image is too coarse both for people to look at and for a detector to work on.

RIM: ray-based inverse mapping

Think of each pixel of the elemental image as re-emitted through a virtual lens array and captured again by a virtual camera. Under telecentric capture and orthographic recapture, this turns into a one-to-one swap of (lens index, position under the lens) into (position under the lens, lens index). There is no interpolation and therefore no quantization error, and it produces as many views as there are pixels under a lens, each with as many pixels as there are lenses. The paper uses the result as the first stage of computational integral imaging reconstruction (CIIR).

iRIM: a virtual lens array from multi-view images

Because RIM is one to one, it also runs backwards. iRIM takes multi-view images and synthesizes the elemental image of the virtual lens array you want. The paper covers a matched 6 × 6 configuration for a 24-inch FHD panel (1.6056 mm lenses, 320 × 180 of them).

How the home page lab is made

The lab computes lens array capture, RIM and reconstruction in the browser and animates them. For the Blender scene and your own images the capture step is a simplified model; the Lytro sample uses data from a real camera.

  • Sample scene: the Blender scene from the ICCAS 2026 paper (one center view), split by object into four layers at different depths: the torus, the monkey head, the cone, sphere and cube, and the background. Each layer is a flat picture rather than a 3D model, so parts that were hidden behind other objects are empty.
  • Capture: a pinhole array model. For each sensor pixel, the ray through its lens center is traced back, and the colour of the first opaque layer it meets is recorded in that elemental-image pixel.
  • Rearrangement and reconstruction: RIM, which swaps (lens index, position under the lens), and reconstruction, which shifts the views by distance and averages them, follow the papers. When the distance matches a layer's depth, that layer's samples land back where they came from and it becomes sharp. On screen the reconstruction plane is drawn at the depth that distance stands for, so an object turns sharp as the plane passes where it stood.
  • Lytro sample: the occlusions_26 scene from the Stanford Lytro Light Field Archive (Raj et al., 2016). From the raw lens-array image of a Lytro Illum (14 × 14 pixels per lens) the central 8 × 8 views are used, at 128 × 128 lenses (64 × 64 on phones). The capture was done by a real camera, so it is not simulated; in step 1 the points stand at the depth where the 64 views agree best. Rearrangement and reconstruction run on the real data.
  • Your own image: a single photo has no depth, so the edge colour is taken as background and the image is split into a subject layer and a background layer. If the background cannot be separated, it stands as one flat card. The image never leaves your browser.
  • Sound: the music, sound effects and robot voice are all synthesized by the browser.
